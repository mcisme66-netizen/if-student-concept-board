# TACTILE CITY case study

This is the reasoning path that produced the reference board in `assets/tactile-city-board-reference.png`.

## 1. Why the direction changed

Earlier ideation repeatedly produced child healthcare, emergency preparedness, elder safety, and sensor-based products. They were easy to visualize but occupied crowded student-award territory.

Research into recent iF student winners showed stronger signals around:

- dignity rather than rescue;
- participation rather than passive assistance;
- low-cost strategic tools rather than unnecessary electronics;
- cultural and ecological relationships;
- a real maintenance or reuse loop.

This led to a deliberate move into **Reduced Inequalities / Public Design**.

## 2. The opportunity

Architectural interpretation is dominated by images and spoken descriptions. Blind and low-vision visitors often receive separate accessibility aids, while sighted visitors remain in a visual-only experience.

The design question became:

> How might different visitors explore the same architecture through touch and leave with a portable record of place?

## 3. System definition

TACTILE CITY was defined as five connected parts:

1. a waist-height public relief station;
2. an interchangeable site plate with real texture, raised façade, simplified plan, direction arrow, and short braille label;
3. a handheld rolling frame;
4. reusable microcell embossing cards;
5. a ring-bound tactile archive plus reverse smoothing roller at a hub.

The critical interaction is not “scan for information.” It is:

```text
orient -> touch the place -> roll one impression -> carry it -> compare places -> reset/reuse
```

## 4. Ethical and practical boundaries

- Blind users are shown as independent participants, not charity recipients.
- Sighted and blind visitors use the same interface.
- The cane stays clear of the circulation path.
- Tactile information is primary; audio is optional and offline.
- The concept makes no claim to replace orientation and mobility systems.
- Long braille passages are avoided because image generators cannot guarantee braille accuracy; final prototypes require expert review.

## 5. Visual identity

- Warm white field.
- Charcoal recycled-polymer rolling frame.
- Terracotta grip and accent rules.
- Bronze-gray public relief station.
- Pale-cream tactile cards.
- Historic masonry scene used to make texture and place immediately legible.

## 6. Board hierarchy

- Upper left: problem and proposition.
- Center: hero system showing station, frame, cards, archive.
- Upper right: blind adult and sighted child exploring one station together.
- Middle: ORIENT / EXPLORE / CAPTURE / COMPARE.
- Lower: route map, card anatomy, reset loop, and three values.
- Footer: category, concept, target, status.

## 7. Why the board reads clearly

- One physical action—rolling a card—anchors the story.
- The permanent station and portable archive create a before/after system.
- Inclusion is visible through shared use, not explained only in text.
- Reuse is shown through the smoothing roller rather than claimed abstractly.
- Product form, public installation, service route, and maintenance are all present on one board.

## 8. What to validate next

- relief depth and minimum distinguishable line spacing;
- embossing-card material and reset cycles;
- rolling force for different ages and grip strengths;
- braille and tactile-map standards with blind users;
- station height, reach range, drainage, vandal resistance, and outdoor aging;
- permissions and maintenance ownership for cultural routes.

When adapting this case, preserve the method—shared interface, tangible action, system loop, explicit boundary—not the architecture subject.
