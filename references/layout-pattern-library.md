# Layout pattern library

Use this reference when selecting a board composition from user-supplied award references. It records derived layout principles, not the products, logos, identities, or exact compositions of those references.

## What the sample set shows

The reviewed boards span older object-led presentations and newer product-service systems. The strongest recurring principle is not a particular red band, font, or grid. It is **one dominant claim supported by two or three visual proofs**.

Recurring strengths:

- one immediately legible hero or use action;
- a simple problem-to-intervention relationship;
- secondary line drawings that explain use without competing with the hero;
- limited color, often one accent against white, grey, or a muted scenario;
- components and steps that preserve the same product identity;
- a quiet entrant strip separated from the design story.

Recurring weaknesses:

- equal-sized modules with no focal point;
- long paragraphs set too small to read;
- decorative technical diagrams that do not prove the claim;
- app screens that overpower the physical interaction;
- repeated medical, rescue, child-safety, or sensor concepts without a new system insight;
- fake test charts, prototype photographs, or impact numbers;
- overly saturated full-board color fields that reduce legibility;
- rendering drift between hero, exploded view, and use sequence.

## Layout families

Choose one primary family. Borrow at most one secondary device from another family.

### A. Hero + mechanism

Best for a compact product with one memorable physical principle.

- Hero render: 40–55% of board.
- Mechanism or section: 15–25%.
- Three-step use sequence: 15–20%.
- Short problem and benefit text: 10–15%.

Use for fold, expand, rotate, tear, filter, lock, latch, cap, or material-transformation concepts. Show the mechanism large enough to understand. Do not add a service map unless the service is essential.

### B. Scenario + intervention

Best when the value is a changed social or spatial interaction.

- Dominant human scenario: 40–50%.
- Product hero: 20–30%.
- Before/after or conflict diagram: 10–20%.
- Two to four details: remainder.

Use for public space, mobility, hospitality, inclusive design, education, and shared facilities. The person and context must show the problem without stigmatizing the user.

### C. Product-service system

Best for several actors, touchpoints, or maintenance responsibilities.

- Hero product or service node: 30–40%.
- Actor flow or three-to-five-step journey: 20–30%.
- Components and states: 15–25%.
- Operator, maintenance, return, or governance loop: 10–15%.

Use for community services, infrastructure, shared products, circular systems, and connected products. Keep app screens subordinate unless the digital interaction is the actual intervention.

### D. Material + lifecycle

Best when material transformation or circularity is the central innovation.

- Finished product family: 35–45%.
- Material source and transformation: 20–30%.
- Assembly or disassembly: 15–20%.
- Recovery, repair, or end-of-life loop: 10–15%.

Show a real loop: who collects, cleans, remanufactures, and returns the material. Do not use green color or recycled texture as proof of sustainability.

### E. Wearable + validation

Best for body-worn or assistive concepts whose fit and behavior matter.

- In-context wearing image: 30–40%.
- Product stack or exploded view: 20–25%.
- Interaction/feedback: 15–20%.
- Evidence or validation plan: 15–25%.

Show donning, reach, pressure points, cleaning, charging, and failure state when relevant. Use real prototype evidence only when supplied. For medical-adjacent work, state that the concept does not diagnose or replace professional care.

## Selection test

Before laying out the board, answer:

1. What should a juror understand in five seconds?
2. Which single image proves that most directly?
3. What are the two missing proofs: mechanism, sequence, system ownership, evidence, or lifecycle?
4. Which content can be removed without weakening the claim?

If no single image can carry the claim, the concept may still be too broad.

## Density and hierarchy checks

- At thumbnail size, the title, hero, and one action must remain legible.
- No more than three visual scales: hero, supporting module, labels/footer.
- Keep at least 8–12% of the active canvas as unoccupied breathing room.
- Use no more than two paragraph blocks; convert the rest into labels, steps, or diagrams.
- Use one photo/render language for the hero and one explanatory language for secondary diagrams.
- Red bands and award-like visual signatures are optional historical cues, not required styling and never permission to place award logos.

## Prompt routing line

Add one of these lines to an ImageGen prompt:

```text
Layout family: Hero + mechanism. One dominant product render, one enlarged mechanism, one three-step sequence; no service map.
```

```text
Layout family: Scenario + intervention. One dominant realistic use scene, a smaller coherent product hero, and a concise before/after conflict diagram.
```

```text
Layout family: Product-service system. One hero node, a three-to-five-step actor flow, consistent components, and a visible maintenance/governance loop.
```

```text
Layout family: Material + lifecycle. Product family first, then material transformation, assembly/disassembly, and a credible recovery loop.
```

```text
Layout family: Wearable + validation. Contextual wearing view, exploded product stack, feedback interaction, and a validation plan without fabricated results.
```
